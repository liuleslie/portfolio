# Hindsight Case Study Refinement Brief

## Purpose

Use this document as durable context while refining the **Hindsight**
portfolio case study.

This brief supersedes the earlier idea that the primary response to C3
AI's feedback should be to manufacture 2--3 new speculative polished UI
interventions.

The better response is to expose the **real breadth of design
exploration and decision-making that already happened during the
project**, while adding enough complete interface views for readers to
understand the product.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Core portfolio goal

Hindsight should demonstrate:

> I can solve difficult technical interaction and
> information-representation problems with expert users.

The case study should make Leslie's design authorship visible through: -
interaction-model decisions - information architecture - representation
choices - exploratory visual thinking - convergence and prioritization -
willingness to abandon interesting ideas when they do not serve the
user - implementation awareness - user evaluation - competent UI craft

The goal is **not** to position Leslie as a pixel-perfect visual/UI
specialist.

The goal is to demonstrate strong product and interaction design
judgment in a technical context.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Context: C3 AI feedback

A C3 AI design reviewer said that Hindsight and Digital Sky Surveys
looked fairly similar. They found the work technical, which they
explicitly said they value, but found it difficult to gauge Leslie's
"design skills and style" because of the projects' sameness and
technical nature.

They suggested that Leslie could create a few screens showing how the
experience might be improved beyond the project's technical constraints.

## Interpretation

Treat the suggestion as an example of how to solve a **missing signal**,
not as a literal requirement to create three speculative redesigns.

The missing signal is:

> What does Leslie do when she has room to design? What is her own
> design judgment, formal sensibility, and range?

The response does NOT need to be: - three new polished SaaS-style
screens - a retrospective design system - invented loading/empty
states - speculative button polishing - a visual reskin

A better answer may already exist in the original Hindsight process.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Key new material to expose: representation explorations

During Hindsight, Leslie explored much more abstract and expressive ways
of representing rover drives before converging on the final operational
interface.

These explorations should become a meaningful part of the case study.

They are useful because they demonstrate:

> Leslie did not arrive at a restrained technical/data interface because
> that is the only visual language she knows.

Instead:

> Leslie explored a much broader representational solution space and
> deliberately converged on direct charts, imagery, and synchronized
> operational data because those representations better served rover
> operators.

This is design judgment.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Exploration 1: movement notation / Labanotation

## Origin

During research, the team read historical material about how rover
operators were trained, including material dating to approximately the
mid-2000s.

This prompted Leslie to investigate whether movement-notation systems
could provide a precedent for encoding rover movement.

One reference was **Labanotation / choreography notation**.

## Exploration

Leslie explored a glyph-based system that attempted to encode
characteristics of a rover drive using a more abstract visual language
influenced by movement notation.

The exploration was intentionally speculative and comparatively wild.

Do not hide this simply because it was rejected early.

## Why it matters

The portfolio value is not:

> "I designed a cool glyph system."

The value is:

> "I investigated whether rover movement could be encoded as a compact
> visual language, then recognized that doing so would introduce an
> additional representational system for operators to learn."

The glyph idea was abandoned relatively quickly.

That rejection is useful evidence of product judgment.

## Important framing

Do NOT apologize for the exploration.

Do NOT imply it was seriously proposed for production if it was not.

Frame it as early representational exploration.

Potential section title:

### How much can a drive be compressed?

or:

### Exploring a visual language for drives

Potential narrative structure:

> Early in the project, we explored whether the characteristics of a
> drive could be compressed into a recognizable visual form. Historical
> accounts of rover-operator training led me to movement-notation
> systems, including Labanotation, as a precedent for encoding motion.

Then show the actual explorations prominently.

Explain what dimensions/qualities Leslie attempted to encode only if
this can be accurately reconstructed from project artifacts/notes.

# Exploration 2: drive signatures

The movement/glyph exploration produced a more durable question:

> Could a drive have a recognizable visual signature without requiring
> operators to learn an entirely new notation system?

This led to experiments around a **drive signature** or profile of a
drive.

Leslie explored different levels/forms of representation, including
approximately:

-   a compact stamp
-   an exploded diagram
-   a more detailed topographic/profile-like representation

Use the actual project terminology and artifacts where possible.

Do not invent a cleaner taxonomy if the artifacts do not support it.

## Why this matters

This sequence can show progression from:

high abstraction → intermediate representation → richer representation →
direct operational data

The important story is not simply that several visuals were created.

Explain what each representation privileged and what it lost, if
supported by the actual process.

Possible questions to answer:

-   What became easier to perceive?
-   What became harder to inspect?
-   How much interpretation did the representation require?
-   Did it support overview but not precise retrieval?
-   Did it obscure the underlying telemetry?
-   What would an operator have to learn to use it?

Only make claims that can be supported by the real project history.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Convergence: choosing directness over abstraction

The expressive explorations were ultimately not needed in the final
operational interface.

The operators primarily needed access to the actual underlying data
visualizations: charts, graphs, imagery, telemetry, etc.

This should be framed as a design conclusion, not a failed experiment.

Potential section:

### Choosing directness over abstraction

Possible narrative direction:

> As we learned more about how operators retrieved and compared previous
> drives, the value of increasingly abstract signatures diminished.
> Operators needed to inspect the underlying telemetry and imagery
> rather than learn another representation of it. The final interface
> therefore kept those data sources direct and synchronized rather than
> collapsing them into a single encoded signature.

This wording is directional. Verify it against actual project evidence
before using it verbatim.

The key conceptual point is:

> Exploration established where abstraction stopped being useful.

That is a strong product-design story.

Do NOT interpret C3's "design skills and style" solely as: - polished
buttons - fashionable UI - signature colors - perfect component

The representation explorations can reveal a deeper formal/design
sensibility:

-   how Leslie thinks about space
-   how Leslie encodes data
-   how Leslie composes information
-   how Leslie uses visual metaphors
-   how Leslie moves between abstraction and utility
-   how Leslie explores broadly before converging

The portfolio should let the reader see that range.

# Positioning: do not overclaim visual-design specialization

Leslie is concerned that "pixel-perfect visual designer" is not an
accurate professional identity.

Do not distort the portfolio to satisfy one employer's preference for
pixel-perfect UI specialists.

Baseline UI competence still matters: - hierarchy - typography -
spacing - states - components - accessibility - consistency

But distinguish:

> I need to demonstrate competent UI craft

from:

> I need to market myself as a visual/UI craft specialist.

The latter is NOT the portfolio goal.

Leslie's stronger interests/skills are closer to:

-   How should this tool be conceptualized?
-   How should someone orient themselves within it?
-   What information belongs together?
-   What representation makes sense?
-   How should the interaction model correspond to the user's mental
    model?
-   What should the system expose or hide?
-   How can complex information become actionable?

This is interaction/product design.

# Refined positioning language

Avoid:

> technical product designer who can still handle craft

because it makes craft sound secondary and slightly defensive.

A better internal positioning direction is:

> Product designer focused on complex tools, interaction models, and
> information-rich systems.

Possible supporting idea:

> I work closely with domain experts and use research, visual
> representation, and code to figure out how unfamiliar systems should
> behave.

Do not treat this exact copy as final.

The emerging differentiator may be less:

> technical + visual

and more:

> technical + interaction + representation

The Communication Design background supports Leslie's ability to
structure and represent information even if Leslie does not identify as
a pixel-perfect visual-design specialist.

# Implementation truthfulness

Hindsight was developed in code and evaluated with relevant users, but
it was NOT yet officially integrated into rover operations.

The team was discussing operational integration.

Do NOT call it: - shipped - deployed - in production

Prefer accurate language such as: - functional implementation -
functional tool - coded prototype - built and tested - under discussion
for operational integration

Some final presentation imagery was composited/Photoshopped for a paper.

Do NOT animate composited imagery in a way that implies it was a live
implementation.

Use: - static sequences - annotated screenshots - actual prototype
artifacts - clear labels

Distinguish: - design proposal - prototype - functional/coded
implementation - operational deployment

# Revised Hindsight case-study structure

Recommended direction:

## 1. Context / problem

Keep short.

Explain the operational problem without excessive Mars-domain
exposition.

## 2. Research insight

How operators remember, retrieve, and compare drives.

Use only research artifacts that affect design decisions.

## 3. Exploring representation

Show:

Labanotation / movement notation → glyph experiments → drive signatures
→ stamp / exploded / topographic forms

Explain what was being tested conceptually.

## 4. Converging on direct data

Explain why abstract encoding was not ultimately necessary/useful
enough.

Show transition into direct charts, telemetry, imagery, etc.

## 5. Retrieval interaction model

Show the two search modes: - conditions - reference drive

## 6. Information architecture

Explain synchronization across: - map - imagery - telemetry - logs -
time

## 7. Testing / iteration

If real evidence exists, show one meaningful:

assumption / V1 → operator feedback → change / V2

Do not add process artifacts merely for convention.

## 8. Final product

Show 2--3 sufficiently complete interface states plus detail crops.

Allow the reader to reconstruct the tool.

## 9. Outcome / status

Accurately describe: - functional coded implementation -
evaluation/testing - relevant observed outcome(s) - discussions around
operational integration

Do not overclaim deployment.
